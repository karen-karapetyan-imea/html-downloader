      A pair of Chinese blue and white salt trenchers with antiquities design, KangxiDia.: 8,6 cm - H.: 3,2 cmProvenance:- Rob Michiels Auctions, 28 April 2018, lot 301. (sold EUR 1.300,00) - The Mr. and Mrs. Baert-Devos Collection, Belgium, acquired from the above. (see added scan)Each of compressed circular form, finely painted in underglaze blue. The wells are decorated with elegant arrangements of auspicious antiquities (bogu), including precious vessels, scrolls, books, and scholarly implements, while the exteriors are encircled by a continuous frieze of further treasured objects alternating with ribbons and floral sprays. The salts have recessed glazed interiors and unglazed foot rims.During the medieval period, salt was a valuable commodity in the West. Dining tables were typically furnished with a single large standing salt or master salt, the position of which reflected the social status of those seated at the table. This gave rise to the expressions 'above the salt', referring to honoured guests, and 'below the salt', referring to those of lower rank or servants.As salt became more affordable around 1700, these large communal salt cellars gradually disappeared. Wealthy households instead adopted individual trencher salts, allowing each guest to season their food from their own salt cellar. By the 1730s, these were largely replaced by oval or cauldron-shaped silver salt cellars raised on three or four feet, often fitted with removable blue glass liners to protect the silver from the corrosive effects of salt. These fashionable European forms were subsequently adapted by Chinese potters for the export market.Condition reports and additional high-resolution zoomable images are available on our website www.rm-auctions.com, or by request via info@rm-auctions.com. Having failed his army entrance exam, he was sent to a private crammer in London to prepare for his Foreign Office entrance, which he never sat. However, it was during these two years that he came into contact with people interested in the study of psychic phenomena. From the middle of the 19th century, a modern spiritualism movement had grown up, propelled by mediums professing the ability to communicate to the dead. Other comparable groups arose, including the Theosophical Society, co-founded in 1875 by Madame Blavatski (1831-1891), combining Eastern Mysticism, which ultimately led to the New Age Movement later in the 20th century, drawing on occultism, spiritualism, New Thought as well as theosophy. In 1875, Haggard was sent by his father to southern Africa to take up an unpaid Government appointment, and in 1877, was present for the announcement of the British annexation of the Boer Transvaal. Haggard married Marianna Margitson in 1880 whilst on a visit to England, the couple briefly returning to South Africa where they had a son, Arthur, in 1881, before returning to England in 1882 to live in Ditchingham. Their son died of measles in 1891, but they had three surviving daughters: Angela, Dorothy and Lilias. In 1884, Haggard was called to the Bar but did little legal work, preferring to spend his time writing novels, such as 'King Solomon's Mines', published in 1885 and said to be the first of the 'Lost World' genre. His book 'She' has proved enduringly popular, selling in excess of 80 million copies. Three of his novels were written in collaboration with his friend Andrew Lang, who shared his interest in the spiritual realm and paranormal phenomena. One of Haggard's greatest friends was Wallis Budge (1857-1934), keeper of Egyptian and Assyrian Antiquities at the British Museum, who exerted considerable influence on Haggard's Egyptological outlook. Psychic investigation, precognitive dreams, occultism and Rosicrucianism were all common areas of interest and association between the two men. Haggard himself was also reputed to be a member of the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn - founded in London by William Wynn Westcott and made famous by the actress Florence Farr, the secret society derived from the failed Hermetic Brotherhood of Egypt, the rituals for which took their inspiration from the Societas Rosicruciana in Anglia. Rosicrucianism and Freemasonry enjoyed many crossovers, and Rider Haggard was also a prominent Freemason. Haggard visited Egypt in 1887, 1904, 1912 and 1924-5, providing inspiration for his many Egyptian-themed novels, and is known to have owned at least thirty-six antiquities, which were largely given to Norwich Museum, with one or two pieces to the British Museum. It is not felt that this carving dates from antiquity, but it is possible that Haggard acquired it on his travels, possibly in Egypt, or it may have been removed to Ditchingham from the structure or decoration of one of the failed secret occult societies to which Haggard subscribed.
